Fiscal Policy Analysis
The One Big Beautiful Bill Act: Fiscal Structure and Distributional Effects
A quantitative assessment of the OBBBA's tax revenue reductions, program spending cuts, and net deficit impact over the FY2025–2034 budget window.
Prepared August 2026
Primary Sources CBO · JCT · BPC · TPC · Tax Foundation
Budget Window FY2025 – FY2034
Executive Summary
A historic tax cut, partially offset by program spending reductions

Signed into law on July 4, 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) represents the most significant federal fiscal legislation since the 2012 American Taxpayer Relief Act. The law permanently extends the individual and business tax provisions of the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) — which were scheduled to expire at the end of 2025 — and layers on a suite of new tax reductions including deductions for tipped income, overtime pay, auto loan interest, and an enhanced senior deduction.

To partially offset those costs, the law enacts the largest cuts to Medicaid, SNAP, and federal student loan programs in modern legislative history. However, the spending reductions cover only approximately 24 cents of every dollar of tax revenue lost, producing a substantial net increase in federal deficits over the decade.

Bottom line: The OBBBA is structurally a large tax cut funded primarily by new federal borrowing. Program spending cuts — concentrated in Medicaid ($917B), student loans ($320B), and SNAP ($187B) — provide a meaningful but insufficient offset. Including estimated interest on incremental borrowing, the total fiscal cost rises to approximately $4.1 trillion over the decade. On a dynamic basis incorporating projected GDP growth of 0.7%, the Tax Foundation estimates the net deficit increase narrows to approximately $3.3 trillion.

Section 2
Tax revenue reductions: structure and composition

The OBBBA reduces federal tax revenues by a net $4.5 trillion over the decade on a current-law basis. The single largest component — individual income tax rate reductions through permanent TCJA extension — accounts for $2.2 trillion, or nearly half of gross tax cuts before any offsets. The standard deduction expansion and AMT relief together add another $1.1 trillion.

Three revenue-raising provisions partially offset gross cuts of $5.2 trillion: the rollback of Inflation Reduction Act clean energy credits ($543B), restrictions on premium tax credit eligibility ($185B), and the retention of the TCJA's repeal of personal exemptions ($190B). These offsets reduce gross cuts to a net $4.5 trillion revenue loss.

The distribution of individual income tax cuts is highly skewed toward upper-income households. The top quintile (incomes above ~$226,000) captures approximately 60% of total benefits, with an average annual tax cut of $12,540 in 2026. By contrast, the bottom quintile receives an average cut of $150 — a ratio of approximately 84:1 in absolute dollar terms.

The top 1% of earners (incomes above roughly $1 million) receive an average annual reduction of $75,410. This reflects the structural mechanics of rate-based cuts: percentage-point reductions in marginal rates deliver larger absolute savings to those with higher taxable incomes.

Key finding: The individual income tax rate cuts — the single largest line item at $2.2 trillion — function through marginal rate reductions that are mathematically regressive in absolute dollar terms. Higher-income households with larger taxable income bases capture proportionally larger savings even when percentage-of-income benefits are similar across the upper-middle and upper quintiles.
Section 3
Program spending reductions: scale and composition

The OBBBA enacts $1.45 trillion in gross spending reductions, offset by $333 billion in new spending on defense and border security, for a net spending reduction of approximately $1.1 trillion. This is the largest set of cuts to federal entitlement programs since the Balanced Budget Act of 1997.

Medicaid reforms — including new work requirements, more frequent eligibility redeterminations, and reductions in provider taxes used to draw down federal matching funds — account for $917 billion, or 63% of gross spending cuts. CBO estimates these changes will result in approximately 11.8 million people losing Medicaid coverage over the decade. Student loan program restructuring ($320B) and SNAP reforms ($187B) account for the remainder of meaningful cuts.

These reductions are concentrated among lower-income households — the same population that receives the smallest absolute benefit from the tax provisions — creating an asymmetric net fiscal impact across the income distribution when both sides of the ledger are considered together.

Section 4
Net deficit impact: $3.4 trillion over the decade

Combining the $4.5 trillion net tax revenue reduction with the $1.1 trillion net spending reduction, the OBBBA increases federal deficits by approximately $3.4 trillion over FY2025–2034 on a conventional (static) basis. This figure rises to approximately $4.1 trillion when BPC's estimate of incremental interest on new borrowing (~$700 billion) is included.

The Tax Foundation's dynamic score, which incorporates an estimated 0.7% increase in long-run GDP from the bill's investment incentives, reduces the conventional figure by roughly $837 billion to approximately $3.3 trillion — meaning economic growth pays for approximately 16% of the gross tax cuts under their model. CBO's dynamic analysis of the House version found the opposite: that the economic drag from higher debt marginally worsened the fiscal outcome relative to the static score.

It is also notable that more than 60% of the law's net cost is concentrated in the first half of the budget window, before temporary provisions (tips, overtime, senior deduction) expire and before some scheduled spending cuts take full effect. A future Congress that extends those expiring provisions without offsetting savings would add approximately $800 billion to the 10-year cost.

Section 5
Conclusions

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act is, in structural terms, the largest tax cut in U.S. history measured as a share of the economy since 1940. Its fiscal mechanics reflect a deliberate policy choice: to make permanent the 2017 TCJA rate reductions and deduction changes — with enhancements — and to partially offset that cost through cuts to means-tested entitlement programs.

Three conclusions follow from the analysis above:

1. The offset is real but insufficient. The program spending cuts are historically significant — Medicaid in particular represents the largest single reduction to a federal entitlement in decades. But they cover only about one-quarter of the tax revenue cost. The remainder is financed by new federal borrowing, adding meaningfully to a debt trajectory already described by CBO as historically unprecedented.

2. The distributional asymmetry is structural. The income tax reductions deliver their largest absolute benefits to upper-income households through the mechanics of marginal rate cuts and expanded deductions. The spending reductions fall most heavily on lower-income households through Medicaid and SNAP. The net distributional effect — combining both sides — is more regressive than either side in isolation.

3. The 10-year window understates the long-run cost. The conventional $3.4 trillion figure reflects a budget window that captures some temporary provisions expiring. If a future Congress extends those provisions — as has consistently occurred with prior expiring tax cuts — the long-run cost could approach or exceed $5 trillion inclusive of interest.
